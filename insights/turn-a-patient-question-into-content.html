How a patient's question becomes a useful piece of content

By Ryno Cilliers · October 9, 2026

Every dental practice has a quiet source of content that nobody schedules: the questions patients ask in the chair.

"Is this normal?" "Do I really need to come back in six months?" "Why does my gum feel different?" Each one is a topic your patients are already curious about, and each one you answer in your own words.

Start with the question as it was asked

Write the question down exactly as the patient phrased it. Patients rarely use clinical terms, and that is useful. It tells you the language your readers use and the worry underneath it.

Answer it the way you would in the room

Say what you would say to the patient. Then read it back and mark anything that sounds like a leaflet. Replace it with the sentence you actually spoke.

Keep the claims modest

Patient education should explain, not promise. Avoid guaranteeing outcomes, and avoid language that suggests one approach is right for everyone. A good piece says what is generally true, says when it depends, and invites the reader to ask.

Have a clinician approve it

Anything clinical should be reviewed and approved by the practitioner before it is published. A writer can shape the explanation, but the clinical judgment belongs to you.

End with one clear next step

A booking link, a phone number or a prompt to bring a question to the next appointment is enough. The piece should leave the reader knowing what to do next without feeling pushed.
