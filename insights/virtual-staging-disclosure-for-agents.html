Virtual staging disclosure: what real estate agents need to know

By Ryno Cilliers · November 19, 2026

Virtual staging digitally adds furniture and decor to photographs of empty rooms. Done well, it helps buyers understand scale and purpose. Done carelessly, it can leave a buyer feeling misled the moment they walk through the door.

The fix is not complicated. It comes down to being clear about what the image is.

Why disclosure matters

A staged photo shows furniture that is not in the home. If a buyer cannot tell, they may form expectations about space, layout or condition that the property cannot meet. Many MLSs and local regulators expect agents to make clear when an image has been virtually staged, and the exact wording and placement differ from market to market.

A simple disclosure habit

Whatever your local rule says, these steps cover most situations:

  • Label each staged image with a plain note such as “Virtually staged”, either on the image or in the caption.
  • Keep the original empty-room photo and offer it to buyers who ask, or include it alongside the staged version.
  • Check your MLS rules and your brokerage policy before publishing, since both may add requirements.

What staging should not change

Staging adds furniture. It should not alter what is permanent: walls, floors, windows, fixtures, room dimensions or the view. Removing a power line, enlarging a room or hiding damage is not staging, and it can create real problems for the agent who publishes it.

Start with a good source photo

Staged images look most believable when the original is clear, well lit and shot straight on with the whole room in frame. A sharp empty-room photo gives a better result than any amount of editing afterwards.

Choosing a service

If you use a staging service, ask whether a person reviews each image, whether permanent features are left unchanged, and what the service expects you to disclose.
